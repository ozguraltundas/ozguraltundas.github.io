1. On Career Pathways

Özgür: What motivated you to pursue a career in crop production management, and how has your academic training influenced the way you approach practical production challenges?

Draft response: Crop production offers a direct connection between biological knowledge and practical outcomes. For someone considering this career, the appeal is the opportunity to see how decisions about soil, water, timing and crop management play out over a season. Academic training provides a useful way to approach those decisions: observe carefully, consider possible explanations and look for evidence before changing the plan. In practice, a field rarely behaves exactly like a textbook example. I would combine that scientific approach with regular field visits and conversations with the people doing the work. Good management depends on understanding both the crop's needs and the team's ability to respond.

2. On Seed Performance

Özgür: How do you evaluate seed vigour, genetic purity, and germination rate together when making decisions about seed growing and production quality?

Draft response: I would treat germination, vigour and genetic purity as complementary measures. A germination test indicates the proportion of seeds capable of producing normal seedlings under specified favourable conditions. Vigour provides additional information about establishment under a wider range of conditions; two lots with similar germination results may behave differently in the field. Genetic purity addresses whether the seed is true to the intended variety. A strong result in one measure does not compensate for a problem in another. Decisions should therefore combine representative testing with production history, storage conditions and the intended sowing environment, rather than relying on a single percentage or visual impression.

3. On Field Decisions

Özgür: How do you adjust production plans when weather, crop performance, or field conditions change, while keeping your team aligned with quality goals?

Draft response: I would begin by checking what has changed through field observations, weather information and recent records. The next step is to identify which operations are time-sensitive and which can be moved without compromising the crop. A revised plan needs to be simple enough for everyone to use: what happens today, who is responsible and what observation would trigger another adjustment? Short team briefings and clear records help keep the field and management teams aligned. After the change, I would check the crop's response rather than assume the adjustment worked. Flexibility is valuable when it is guided by observations and shared quality goals.

4. On Professional Development

Özgür: For a young graduate entering crop or seed production, which practical experiences and skills would you prioritise during the first few years of their career?

Draft response: The first priority would be to gain experience across the whole production cycle, from field preparation and sowing to harvest and storage. I would also spend time with seed testing and quality teams to understand how field decisions appear in the final results. Practical skills include recognising crop growth stages, taking representative samples, maintaining useful field records and working confidently with spreadsheets. Communication deserves equal attention: a good observation has limited value if it never reaches the person who can act on it. A simple learning journal can help connect actions with outcomes and turn each season into a more useful learning experience.
