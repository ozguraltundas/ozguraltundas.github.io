Hasan Hüseyin Dede’s contribution to Episode 02 focuses on seed production, seed quality, and genetic purity.

1. On Career Pathways

Özgür: How did your academic background shape your career in seed production, and what was the most important lesson you learned when moving from university into industry?

Draft response: An agricultural education provides a foundation for understanding genetics, plant development and the conditions that affect a crop. In seed production, that knowledge becomes useful when it helps explain what is happening in a field and what action should come next. I would describe the transition to industry as learning to connect scientific reasoning with timing, resources and teamwork. A technically sound plan still needs clear responsibilities and consistent follow-up. The most useful lesson for a new graduate is to stay curious: listen to experienced field staff, keep reliable records and compare the outcome of a decision with the reasoning behind it.

2. On Seed Quality

Özgür: From planning a seed production season to harvesting and handling the seed, which decisions have the greatest impact on maintaining seed quality and genetic purity?

Draft response: Seed quality needs attention throughout the production cycle. I would start with a reliable source of seed, a suitable field and a plan for maintaining varietal identity. Depending on the crop, isolation and the timely removal of off-type plants help protect genetic purity. Clear lot labels and clean equipment reduce the risk of mixing during harvest and processing. Harvest timing, careful handling, appropriate drying and suitable storage conditions help preserve the seed's ability to perform. Genetic purity and physiological quality are related parts of the same goal, but they need different checks. Testing should support the production records, with any unexpected result investigated before a lot moves forward.

3. On Production Management

Özgür: When production targets and quality requirements compete, how do you organise your team and decide which priorities should guide the work?

Draft response: I would establish the quality requirements before setting the daily production targets, so the team knows which checks cannot be skipped. Each stage should have a responsible person, a clear handover and a way to report problems early. When time or capacity becomes limited, the first step is to understand the bottleneck: is it labour, equipment, weather or a lot that needs further evaluation? The plan can then be adjusted by changing the sequence of work or reallocating resources. A questionable lot should remain separate while it is assessed. Producing more seed is only useful when its identity and quality remain dependable.

4. On Professional Development

Özgür: What advice would you give to students who want to build a career in the seed industry, and which skills should they develop beyond their academic training?

Draft response: I would encourage students to follow a seed crop through as much of a complete season as possible, including the work after harvest. Time in the field, processing facility and testing laboratory helps connect concepts that are often taught separately. Beyond technical knowledge, accurate record-keeping, basic data analysis and clear communication are valuable habits. Learn to explain an observation, ask a focused question and report a problem while there is still time to respond. It also helps to understand how growers, production teams and quality staff depend on one another. Early in a career, reliability and a willingness to learn can matter as much as having an immediate answer.

Background reading for the seed-quality draft: FAO Seed Production Manual.
